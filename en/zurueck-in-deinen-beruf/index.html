What It Really Takes to Get Your Foreign Degree Recognized in Germany—Honestly, With Numbers and Sources

As of August 2026

You studied or completed vocational training in your home country. In Germany, you’re cleaning, making deliveries, or working as an unskilled laborer in the very field where you’re actually a skilled professional. This page explains what stands between you and your profession—with statistics, sources, and no sugarcoating.

What does it mean to work below your level of qualification?

Working below your qualifications means you’re performing a job that requires a lower level of education than the one you hold. Experts call this “dequalification” or “overqualification.” A doctor working as a nursing assistant and an engineer working in a warehouse are typical examples. The degree itself doesn’t disappear—it just isn’t reflected in income and position.

The difference is measurable in euros. The Bertelsmann study from July 2026 shows that immigrants in Germany earn 19.6 percent less on average, and 36 percent of them work as unskilled laborers. Among people from countries of origin where asylum seekers come from, the figure is 45 percent.

How many immigrants in Germany work in jobs below their qualifications?

About one-third of highly qualified working immigrants in Germany are not working in jobs commensurate with their qualifications. That is nearly twice as many as among those born in Germany, and the proportion has risen by 7 percentage points over the past ten years. The figure comes from the OECD, based on the 2021 EU Labor Force Survey.

The decisive factor here is not immigration itself, but where the degree was earned. According to the OECD, migrants with a foreign degree are 28 percentage points less likely to hold a highly skilled job than immigrants with a German degree. So the piece of paper doesn’t count—regardless of how capable someone is.

Why isn’t my foreign degree sufficient?

A foreign degree does not automatically grant access to a profession in Germany; rather, it is a document that must first be compared to the German educational system. This comparison is called an equivalency assessment. Only the result of this assessment determines whether you are permitted to practice your profession—in the case of regulated professions—or whether your degree is officially recognized as equivalent to a German one.

The process ends positively far more often than many people fear. Of the applications decided in Germany in 2024, 43 percent resulted in full equivalence, 45 percent in recognition with conditions, and 10 percent in partial equivalence. Only 1 percent were rejected. These figures are taken from the 2024 Recognition Statistics published by the Federal Statistical Office.

The number of recognitions has more than tripled since 2016: from 26,200 to 79,100 in 2024. Anyone starting the process today is therefore following a path that tens of thousands of people successfully take each year.

The strongest reason to see it through: Once recognition is granted, the long-term probability of employment increases by about 25 percentage points, and earnings rise by about 20 percent. This was documented by the Institute for Employment Research (IAB) in IAB Brief Report 2/2021.

What role does German really play—and what role does it not play?

In regulated professions, German is a strict, testable gatekeeper, and in unregulated professions, it is necessary but not sufficient. This distinction is important because it determines how much a language test actually accomplishes for you. In nursing and medicine, a specific language proficiency level is required by law; without it, you simply cannot practice the profession.

Outside of regulated professions, language skills only partially solve the problem. The OECD notes that even with an advanced language level, a 18-percentage-point disadvantage remains. The Bertelsmann study from July 2026 also shows that 29 percent of immigrants with recognized degrees still do not work in jobs commensurate with their qualifications.

To be honest, this means: German opens the door, but it doesn’t guarantee that someone will let you through. The OECD explicitly points out that German employers often do not accept foreign qualifications, even in unregulated professions. Knowing this in advance allows you to plan more realistically—and spares you the disappointment of assuming that a language certificate would automatically open doors.

Regulated or unregulated profession: which applies to me?

In a regulated profession, practicing the profession without recognition is prohibited by law; in an unregulated profession, you may start working immediately. This is the first and most important step in any recognition process, because it determines whether recognition is mandatory or voluntary.

How much does recognition cost, and who pays?

The administrative fees alone range from about 40 euros to 600 euros, depending on the federal state and profession; the evaluation of academic records by the Central Office for Foreign Education (ZAB) costs 208 euros. In addition, there are costs for translations, certifications, and obtaining documents from your country of origin. Important: Fees apply even if your application is denied.

The federal government provides a recognition grant to cover these costs. It covers processing fees ranging from 100 to 600 euros, certificate evaluation and qualification analysis up to 1,200 euros, and qualification measures up to 3,000 euros. Since January 1, 2025, the income limit has been 32,000 euros for individuals and 50,000 euros for joint tax filers. The program runs through 2027, and enrollment is open until June 30, 2027.

There are two points where many people miss out on funding. First: The application for the recognition grant must be submitted before the application for recognition—there is no retroactive funding. Second: The recognition grant does not cover language courses or language exam fees. It does not cover the very costs that matter most to you.

For German language courses specifically, there’s a separate program: the BAMF’s vocational language courses under Section 45a of the Residence Act. Anyone earning no more than 20,000 euros per year—or 40,000 euros for joint tax filers—or receiving benefits under SGB II or SGB III can participate free of charge. Above this threshold, each class unit costs 2.56 euros; a course with 400 class units thus costs 1,024 euros, and one with 600 units costs 1,536 euros. Upon passing the exam, 50 percent is refunded upon request.

The recognition counseling offered through the IQ support program is also free and available regardless of income. It helps you determine which agency is responsible for your profession and what documents you need—before you spend money on translations that may not even be required.

How long does the recognition process take?

The statutory deadline for the initial decision is four months, but it does not begin until your documents are complete. For EU degrees with automatic recognition, 87 percent of the procedures are completed within three months. If compensatory measures are required, it takes an average of about 15 months to receive the final decision, according to data from the Federal Institute for Vocational Education and Training (BIBB, data from 2017 to 2020).

The realistic timeframe for a third-country degree with additional requirements is therefore 12 to 24 months from application to receipt of the diploma. IAB Research Report 22/2024 shows just how protracted the second half of the process can be: In half of the cases with additional requirements, up to 426 days elapsed between the initial deficiency notice and the final decision alone.

This waiting period is not time wasted, but rather the very window during which language skills are developed. Those who begin learning German when they submit their application often have one to two years until the remedial measure is required—more than enough time if they study regularly.

What can I actually accomplish alongside a full-time job?

More than most people assume—because the support structure is explicitly designed for working adults. All of the BAMF’s vocational language courses are suitable for employees: They are part-time, with a maximum of 25 class units per week; there are evening and afternoon courses; and virtual formats are available. For people who are currently working below their level of qualification, this is precisely the key point.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need to have my foreign degree recognized in Germany?

That depends on the profession. In regulated professions such as nursing, medicine, pharmacy, psychotherapy, or teaching, recognition is mandatory—without it, you’re not allowed to practice the profession. In unregulated professions such as IT, business administration, or chemistry, you can start working right away; recognition is voluntary and serves as proof for employers.

Is recognition worth it financially?

According to the IAB Brief Report 2/2021, once recognition is granted, the probability of employment increases by about 25 percentage points in the long term, and earnings rise by about 20 percent. This is the most reliable measurement on this issue in Germany.

How much does the recognition process cost?

Depending on the federal state and profession, processing fees range from about 40 euros to 600 euros. The ZAB’s credential evaluation costs 208 euros. Translation and certification fees are additional. Fees are also charged in the event of a rejection—in Bavaria, for example, the 40-euro fee for the certificate.

How long does a recognition process take?

The statutory deadline for the initial decision is four months from the date the documents are deemed complete. According to the BIBB, if a compensatory measure is required, it takes an average of about 15 months to reach a final decision. Realistically, you should plan on 12 to 24 months for a third-country degree with a requirement.

Are recognition applications often rejected?

No. In 2024, only 1 percent of the procedures ended in a rejection. 43 percent resulted in full equivalence, 45 percent in recognition with conditions, and 10 percent in partial equivalence. So the most common response is not “no,” but “yes” with conditions.

Can I submit an application in multiple federal states at the same time?

No. Simultaneous applications to multiple agencies are not permitted. You must choose one competent agency—ideally the one in the federal state where you want to work. North Rhine-Westphalia additionally requires a connection to the state, such as an employment contract, verifiable job applications, or a business plan.

Does the recognition grant cover my German language course?

No. The recognition grant covers procedural costs, the evaluation of certificates, and qualification measures, but explicitly does not cover language courses or language exam fees. For German, the BAMF’s vocational language courses under Section 45a of the Residence Act are the right option.

Is a good command of German enough for me to return to my profession?

In regulated professions, German is a major hurdle that many fail to clear—in these fields, passing a language certification makes a huge difference. In non-regulated professions, German is necessary but not sufficient: According to the OECD, even with an advanced language level, a disadvantage of 18 percentage points remains.

Can I complete the program while working full-time?

Yes. The BAMF’s vocational language courses are specifically designed for employed individuals: part-time options, a maximum of 25 class units per week, evening and afternoon courses, as well as virtual formats are available. For shift workers, the virtual course is often the only practical option.

Do I have to be recognized before entering the country?

Not necessarily. Since March 2024, the recognition partnership under Section 16d(3) of the Residence Act has allowed entry without prior professional recognition. The process is then completed in collaboration with the employer in Germany. This provision is part of the second phase of the Skilled Workers Immigration Act.

Do I always need a certificate evaluation from the ZAB?

Not always. First, check the anabin database of the Conference of Ministers of Education and Cultural Affairs for free. If your degree is listed there with the status H+ and the note “corresponds,” in many cases you won’t need a paid credential evaluation. This saves you 208 euros and several months of waiting time.

Which professions are most frequently recognized in Germany?

Nursing is by far the number one profession: In 2024, 32,500 of the 79,100 positive recognition decisions were for registered nurses, accounting for 41 percent. This is followed by doctors with 11,000, engineering professions with 4,400, teachers with 2,800, and physical therapy with 2,200.

What’s next for you?

The next step depends on your profession. For nursing and healthcare professions, there are specific language requirements and a separate examination pathway via the telc Deutsch B1·B2 Pflege. For academic professions, the first consideration is whether your profession is regulated, followed by the evaluation of your credentials.
